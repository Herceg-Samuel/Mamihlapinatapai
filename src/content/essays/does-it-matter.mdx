At (17:21 - 24:04), Professor Sandel warns that studying political philosophy as an exercise in self-knowledge carries significant **personal and political risks**. He explains that:

- **Philosophy unsettles us:** It takes familiar concepts and makes them "strange," which can be an unsettling experience because once you see things differently, you cannot go back to your previous way of thinking.
- **It can be a distancing activity:** He warns that philosophy can potentially make one a "worse citizen" before it makes them a better one, as it distances students from conventional, established assumptions and settled beliefs.
- **It is unavoidable:** Despite these risks and the temptation to retreat into skepticism, he argues that we cannot avoid these questions because we live out our answers to them every day.

#### Case 1

In the first case, you are the driver of a runaway trolley and must choose whether to stay on track and kill five workers or steer onto a side track to kill one (0:33-1:32).

#### Case 2

In the second case, you are a bystander on a bridge who could stop the trolley by pushing a large man onto the tracks, sacrificing him to save the five workers (4:34-5:37).

#### Types of moral reasoning found

Two main types of moral reasoning:

- **Consequentialist moral reasoning:** This focuses on the results of an action, such as the principle that it is better for five people to live even if one must die (13:30-14:00).
- **Categorical moral reasoning:** This suggests that certain actions are intrinsically wrong, regardless of the consequences, such as the idea that killing an innocent person is never permissible (14:30-15:00).
