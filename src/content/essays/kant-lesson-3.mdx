# Part 3

7. "good" can be extremely bad

Kant argues that the "good" stuff of life can become extremely bad, without a good will.

Talents of mind, temperances and gifts of fortune are and can be good but since in
and of themselves goodness does not exist, they can be extremely bad.

A genius may decide to harm people in clever ways, in seeking happiness, it can be done at the
cost of other things or even people and wealth & money we already know can corrupt. The love of money is the root of all evil.

Kant pushes for the goodness of the good will, and at that we ought aim.

In extrapolation, if a person has a good will or a good heart(in the Biblical sense),
out of it, abundance flows. Good flows from good. (Good tree, good fruit, bad tree, bad fruit) And in a lot of ways, striving for good ceases, as it becomes one's nature to do good.

It becomes as though one cannot help it but do good deeds as goodness now resides in him.
This is in contrast to the existing predisposition to bad. An inward change occurs and actions proceed as always, only
this time by the good!(some will talk of it being achieved through God, others outside Him)

I can see how this bleeds into Aristotle's virtue ethics!

8. No imperatives for the divine will

Firstly, Kant does not have belief in God. Secondly, he believes that we cannot know
about the existence or lack thereof.

God's existence cannot be shown in the synthetic a priori. We cannot prove or disprove God as He is not an object of
possible experience. He argues that we don't "know" God, He is postulated as a necessary being for morality(and other things as mentioned by Spinoza(coming soon))
We can live as though God exists but not in knowledge of its truth(in theoretical terms).
(This is carefully and greatly explained in one of
his books in the Critique collections, in relation to : the synthetic, a priori, empirical and analytic.)

<Quote>
  "I had to deny knowledge in order to make room for faith"
  <Caption>Kant in Critique of Practical Reason</Caption>
</Quote>

Anyway, he still considers this position. He states that the divine will cannot have
imperatives. The "ought" becomes "is" for the divine.

The ought is out of place, the would is already of itself necessarily in agreement with the law.

9. Humans are ends in themselves, never means only

This has to be the greatest one.
Kant would die on this hill.

He believes that insofar as humans have freedom and free will,
they cannot by any means be taken as means only but end in themselves.

Let's define these terms as Kant did:

<Caption>
  1. will: faculty that determines itself to action 2. end: serves the will as
  the objective ground of its self determination 3. means: what contains merely
  the ground for the possibility of the action, whose effect is an end 4.
  incentive: subjective ground for desire 5. motive: objective ground of
  volition
</Caption>

"Means only" is a crucial component. Humans can be means to an end but never means only.
For example, we use teachers as means to learn concepts, doctors as means for treatment.

These examples show them as means but not means only. The people involved have out of their own
volition given themselves for the purposes or ends in question eg learning and treatment.

He opposed slavery as this would in all ways treat humans as means only to some
further end. No freedom and free will is acquitted to the slave.

A modern outlook would be "tools". People are not, should not and ought not(in the imperative sense)
be tools for use for anyone or anything(most of all other humans).
Going back to lesson 7, the three aforementioned are tools and not the thing in itself(the end
being happiness, or as said by Aristotle, flourishing).
