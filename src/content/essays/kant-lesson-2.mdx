# Part 2

4. Actions and moral worth

All actions have moral worth or lack thereof when matters ethics and morality are taken into account.

Kant takes a more stern position as to ascertain that very few kinds of actions have any moral worth.

And as moral worth in this manner is either present or absent, many of actions acclaimed are found deeply wanting.

According to him, an action has moral worth if and only if it is taken out of duty.

What does this mean? An action is taken out of duty if and only if it is done out of respect for the law. That and only that.

Another kind of actions that seeks such credit are those undertaken in accordance to duty. They are those that walk besides duty but never duty in themselves. And given our previous condition, these actions too, regardless of their fruitfulness, fall short!

Kant would prefer you do the right thing for the sake of the right thing even if it means that while in the unpleasant pursuit you curse your being and count even passing second than you doing it happily for it brings you joy that you are able to do such. The man who chooses a life of suffering for the sake of the law and forsakes the shortening of his life has his actions perceived as having moral worth. He that does is good deed, say give happily to charity is chastised. This man did what was good for him, what brought him joy, and not for the sake of duty, duty to the law.

This is a standard I found farfetched and deeply disturbing. I did not find sufficient the reason he gave for such a narrow standard. Even the careful observers of the law(Israelites) had easier times than these!

I understand the epistemological difference of the two but the validity thereof has been lost on me. If bad reasons were persecuted perhaps I would join the mob with shoutings and unrelenting proclamations for respect for the law and for actions to be done from duty.

Moral worth is to be sought. Not in this way however. This is but harassment of actions!

5. Think of others(universal laws)

This is more of an extrapolation. When universalizing a maxim, one is forced out of selfishness and zooming out is necessitated. One looks at the larger picture and the entire human race is at the field of view.

It may however be selfishness in itself; for one has to consider the same deeds effected on him by another, and by loathing this reality, one chooses a better maxim to follow and consequently better, higher laws are birthed, love and concern for self lurking in the background, a dictator of laws.

6. Respect for the law

This follows from the previous statements made.

The law as Kant shows is not abstract. It is the carefully reasoned and universalized maxims that are well regarded and fit for the intended purpose and works in sync with the good will. This is shown to be true as the laws are derivatives of a good will, that which is good in and of itself.

And what comes from good is also good. And in this the law, in its nature is to be respected.

Kant would argue that no law would be bad or evil but if it were the case, he'd stand firm in this position. The law is good(as are good actions) not because of its consequences but rather because it is good.

Therefore, the law ought be respected!
