# Part 1

1. Nothing is good(without qualification)

We can go through the philosophical hell hole of defining the
good but I'll spare us the gruesome burden and wave our hands on
this matter and take it as a polite presupposition that we
understand what good means(mostly given that we are not concerned
of the good at this very moment), even at the base level without
the careless or careful exposition which is far from the core
of this.

Kant argued that nothing is good in itself or even out of itself without qualification.
Only a good will was exempted.

This gives a humility of sorts and a reminder that goodness does not necessarily exist in us.

We are but partakers of this benevolence! It is but a privilege. Yet we live as if it is ours
from the very beginning and from it have we been distanced and like a faithful lover we seek our return, to them we swore we'd rather die if without.
Far from it is. It is a gift we have received and from generation to generation it is passed like a candle
full of the dimming, men careful not to let the light go off, for from whence shall we get another!

Inasmuch as Kant distances himself from God, his works point
towards Him as detailed by William Lane Craig. God is the only one that is good. The goodness is
found in Himself. From Him goodness flows. It is His nature.

This reminds us to rely on Him.

In Mark 10:18 "So Jesus said to him, “Why do you call Me good? No one is good but One, that is, God."

This was revealing coming from Jesus. He reminds us that only God is good!

Kant did not believe in God.

With regards to the goodness of a good will, that I am not
convinced. It feels like a standard that exists of goodness, a
form with no content. It is but a potential with no actuality.

I am aware that Kant nonetheless shows us that a good will is good in and of itself(We shall get to that soon)

I am not sure about this. Reading Kant in his entirety would enlighten this naive wanting understanding of mine.

2. Duty first

Kant emphasizes that duty is key.

We have a duty to the law. A duty to always tell the truth independent of the preying consequences.

Socrates demonstrated this well. He chose to follow the law even to death, even in its "unfair" manner that was bestowed upon him.

He was falsely accused as detailed by Plato. He believed that if he did not abide by the law, then others wouldn't and that would be no way for the law or the people of Athens to be treated.

He obviously did not read Kant but this shows the validity of the statement Kant made.
By validity I do not wish to use Socrates as proof of what Kant claimed, Socrates is
but a humble example I could think of that shows ones devotion to the law, by all means!
It is proper to make the law better than to disobey it because we find it “unjust”.

This feels like a noble pursuit, I'm not sure of its validity(its holding) when the law improperly holds one by the throat. It is however a good rule of thumb and in the same manner well applicable.

3. Focus on actions never the consequences

We are only liable for the actions we choose, never the consequences therein.

Even when we or people we love are in danger, we ought do the right thing for the sake of it being the right and not for the consequences, good or bad.

Actions undertaken with good consequences in mind, however good the action, they possess no moral worth.

The actions become prudent. Prudence is not bad. It can however never be conflated as having moral worth. It will be action in accordance with duty. That and only that! It is not done from duty.

When consequences are bad, we are to follow suit with the right deeds as that and only that lies within our finite control. The terrible consequences are the blood of our perpetrators and not ours.

Focus on own actions at all times!
